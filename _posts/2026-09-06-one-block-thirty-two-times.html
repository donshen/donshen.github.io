The short version

  • A looped transformer runs the same weights several times instead of stacking new ones. You get depth without paying for parameters.
  • It works better than I expected. Open 1.4 B looped models are trading punches with 12 B baselines.
  • It is old. Universal Transformers did this in 2018, and people are right to say so.
  • The real costs are in serving, not in the architecture: your KV cache grows with depth, batching breaks, and the loop order decides your memory bandwidth.
  • Loop counts are going down as models get bigger, not up. That reframes most of the safety argument.
  • What is publicly known about Astra's architecture is essentially one sentence from one OpenAI researcher. Everything else is journalism or inference.

#Depth you can rent

Last week The Information reported that OpenAI's Astra uses a looped transformer. The internet had opinions immediately, and I realised I could not defend a position either way. So I went and read the literature. This is what I found, roughly in the order that made it click.

Start with why depth matters at all, because everything else rests on it. Depth is what lets a transformer do one thing after another. A 32-layer model gets 32 chances to compose a result before it has to commit to a token, and if your problem genuinely needs 60 sequential steps, no amount of width or context will rescue it in a single forward pass.

The usual way to buy those steps is to add layers, each with its own weights. Parameters and depth go up together, which is convenient, because parameters also buy something completely different: somewhere to put facts.

Looping decouples the two. Store k layers, run them L times, and get roughly kL layers of serial computation out of k layers of storage. You pay in compute and in memory traffic, but not in parameters. Saunshi and colleagues put the claim about as sharply as it can be put: many reasoning problems "require a large depth but not necessarily many parameters", and a k-layer transformer looped L times "nearly matches the performance of a kL-layer non-looped model" while being far better than the k-layer model it was built from arXiv:2502.17416.

Drag the slider below and watch the two numbers come apart. That gap is the entire pitch. One convention for the rest of the post: teal marks an early iteration and amber a late one, so the diagrams warm up as the loop runs on.

#Where I got it wrong first

My first mental model was a for loop wrapped around a whole transformer. That is not it, and the difference turns out to matter.

There are three pieces, and only the middle one loops. A prelude embeds the input, a recurrent core iterates, a coda reads out.

e = P(x)   prelude: embed into latent space
s0 ~ N(0, σ2I)   random start, not zeros
si = R(e, si-1)   core, for i = 1 .. r
p = C(sr)   coda: un-embed to logits

Look at the third line again. R takes two arguments. The embedded input goes back in at every single step, not just the first, through an adapter that maps the concatenation of the running state and the input back down to the hidden size.

This is the detail I would have skipped, and it is load-bearing. Geiping et al. are blunt about it: if e were supplied only at the start, "the iterative process would not be stable." An unanchored loop drifts. An anchored one has somewhere to converge to. If you only remember one implementation detail from this post, remember that the loop needs to keep seeing the question.

Notice what is absent: there is no hidden state carried across token positions. The recurrence runs vertically, inside one forward pass, and then it is thrown away. That matters later, because nothing accumulates across a conversation the way it would in an RNN, which is the strongest thing you can say in the architecture's defence.

#What the state does while it loops

This is the part I found genuinely delightful, and it is where the abstract stops being enough.

Iterating a fixed map on a state that is anchored to an input is the setup for a fixed point, and most tokens behave exactly that way. The state settles, and further loops change almost nothing. But when Geiping et al. actually traced the trajectories in PCA space, they found three regimes rather than one. Flip between them below.

That last one is my favourite finding in the whole literature. Nobody built the model a step counter. It grew one out of geometry, because it needed to know how far along it was.

Path independence is what makes this usable

Because s0 is random noise and the model still lands in the same place, the answer does not depend on where you started. That is what buys you the freedom to train at a mean of 32 loops and evaluate at 64.

It also gets you per-token early exit almost for free. Huginn's rule is just the KL divergence between the output distributions of two successive steps: "if this divergence falls below 5×10-4, we stop iterating, sample the output token, and move to generate the next token." Easy tokens leave after a few steps, hard ones keep going, and nobody had to label which is which. #Three tricks that make it trainable

Naively unrolling 32 blocks and backpropagating through all of them would cost you 32 layers of stored activations. None of the working recipes do that, and the three things they do instead are all a little counterintuitive.

Sample the loop count. Huginn draws r per sequence from a log-normal Poisson with mean 32, a distribution that "most often samples values less than r̄, but contains a heavy tail." The model is therefore forced to be correct at many different depths, which is exactly the property you want to exploit at test time. The training distribution is the feature.

Truncate the gradient. "We backpropagate through only the last k iterations of the recurrent unit", with k = 8. The first 24 steps run forward with nothing stored. Training memory stops scaling with r, which is the whole reason this is affordable.

Start from noise. Random s0, a truncated normal with σ2 = 2/5, is what buys the path independence from the previous section. Initialise at zeros and you get a model that only works at the depth you trained it at.

#How far it goes, and where it breaks

Parameters and data were the two knobs everyone knows. Recurrence is a third, and unlike the other two you can turn it after training. Huginn "can improve its performance on reasoning benchmarks, sometimes dramatically, up to a computation load equivalent to 50 billion parameters", from a 3.5 B model.

The 2026 results are what actually moved me. Ouro, an open family of looped language models trained on 7.7 T tokens, reports 1.4 B and 2.6 B models that "match the results of up to 12B SOTA LLMs across a wide range of benchmarks." Parcae, which stabilises looping by constraining the spectral norms of the injection parameters, gets a 1.3 B model to "up to 87.5%" the quality of "a Transformer twice the size."

Then I hit the caveat that spoiled the happy story, and it is the most practically important thing in this post: the curve is not monotone.

So if you go and play with one of these models, sweep test-time r and find your task's peak. Do not assume more is more.

#Reasoning wants depth, facts want parameters

The most useful result in this literature is a dissociation, and once you see it the architecture stops looking like a trick and starts looking like a claim about what reasoning is.

Looped models are good at what depth buys and bad at what parameters buy. The intuition is that a reasoning problem with an iterative solution, like carrying digits or running p-hop induction, needs a number of sequential steps but reuses the same operation at each one. That is a loop. Storing a fact needs somewhere to put it, and a looped model has k layers of somewhere no matter how long it runs.

Ouro's ablations land in the same place from the other direction: the advantage comes from "superior knowledge manipulation capabilities" rather than from increased knowledge capacity. The model does not know more. It does more with what it knows. Saunshi et al. take the dissociation seriously enough to build a "looping-based regularization" out of it.

There is a wrinkle here that took until 2026 to surface, and it undercuts the "just a tweak" reading of all this. Dense looped models do not actually scale. "Looped-MoE models scale better than the standard baseline while dense looped models do not" arXiv:2605.09165, because routing hands you different experts on each pass through the shared layers, "recovering expressivity without additional parameters." Weight tying costs you expressivity, and sparsity is how the field is buying it back. That is not a small architectural detail.

#Against chain of thought

This is the comparison everyone actually wants, and I think it comes down to bandwidth.

A chain-of-thought step pushes its result through the vocabulary: about 17 bits for a 128 k vocabulary, one discrete choice, committed and unrecoverable. A loop passes a hidden state instead: thousands of floats, uncommitted, able to hold several partial hypotheses at once. Saunshi et al. prove looped models "implicitly generate latent thoughts and can simulate T steps of CoT with T loops", so a loop is at least as expressive per step, and it is not restricted to reasoning that happens to be sayable in English.

 Chain of thoughtRecurrent depth
Compute axisSequence length, horizontalLayer count, vertical
Step bandwidthOne token, ~17 bits, discretisedA hidden state, thousands of floats
Training dataWants reasoning tracesNone special, ordinary pretraining

BillingReasoning tokens are billedLoops are not tokens, so not billed

Two footnotes worth having. The horizontal cousin of all this is Coconut, which feeds the last hidden state back as the next input embedding rather than decoding it. Same instinct about the vocabulary bottleneck, opposite axis: it spends sequence positions instead of layers.

And that "contested faithfulness" row is doing real work in the safety argument. If readable traces were faithful records of computation, moving reasoning into latent space would be a clear loss of oversight. But the filler-token result in Let's Think Dot by Dot and the position paper Stop Anthropomorphizing Intermediate Tokens both suggest we were reading tea leaves to begin with. I do not think that settles the question, but it weakens "we are losing the trace" considerably.

#The part that changed my mind

I came in thinking the interesting question was whether looping is a real architectural advance. I left thinking that is the boring question. The interesting one is what it does to your serving stack, and almost none of the online argument touched it.

Your KV cache multiplies. Every iteration is a fresh set of attention layers with its own keys and values, so cache memory grows linearly with reasoning depth. Huginn's mitigation is a fixed cache budget overwritten cyclically, and a budget of 4 cost them surprisingly little. MELT goes further with one gated KV cache per layer shared across loops, reaching "constant-memory iterative reasoning without sacrificing LoopLM performance."

Batching breaks. Once tokens exit at different depths they stop marching through the model together, and your batch falls apart. Continuous depth batching reschedules at the granularity of individual loop iterations and recovers "up to 99% of the theoretical maximum speed-up from adaptive-depth", at 1.5 to 1.9× offline throughput on Ouro 1.4 B and Huginn 3.5 B. Without something like it, adaptive depth is a benchmark result rather than a deployment.

Loop order decides your memory bandwidth. This is the subtlety most summaries miss, and it inverts the popular "great for local inference" claim.

And reinforcement learning needs new machinery. Standard post-training rewards output tokens, which is a category error when the reasoning never becomes a token. LoopRPT attacks this by assigning reinforcement signal to latent steps via an EMA teacher and noisy latent rollouts, and reports gains concentrated on hard tokens rather than on earlier exits. I would treat the RL story as open.

Put together, the picture is a model that is cheaper in parameters, more expensive in cache, awkward to batch, sensitive to loop order, and not yet straightforward to post-train. That is a real engineering programme, not a tweak.

#How we got here

Everyone pointing out that this is not new is correct. What is new is that it survives contact with scale, and that the surrounding engineering finally exists.

  1. 2018
    Universal Transformers

    Dehghani et al. iterate one shared block over all positions and add per-position halting through adaptive computation time. Turing-complete under assumptions. Almost everything since is a variation on this.

  2. 2019
    ALBERT and deep equilibrium models

    Cross-layer parameter sharing for its own sake, and separately the fixed-point view of a weight-tied layer with constant-memory implicit gradients. The second is the better lens for what looping is doing.

  3. 2023
    Looped transformers as programmable computers

    Loops as instruction cycles, and the theory of what depth recurrence can express.

  4. 2024
    Length generalisation

    Fan et al. show looped transformers with an adaptive number of steps generalise to unseen input lengths on tasks with known iterative solutions. Matching iterations to problem size is the whole point.

  5. Feb 2025
    Huginn and the depth-not-parameters claim

    The first serious pretrained proof of concept at 3.5 B and 800 B tokens, alongside the theory that looping simulates chain of thought and that reasoning and memorisation come apart.

  6. Jul 2025
    Mixture-of-Recursions

    Routers assign per-token recursion depth, attention runs only over still-active tokens, and only their KV pairs get cached. Parameter sharing and adaptive compute in one framework.

  7. Oct 2025
    Ouro

    Looping moves into pretraining at 7.7 T tokens with an entropy-regularised objective for learned depth allocation. Open weights, and the strongest evidence that this is not a toy.

  8. 2026
    The engineering year

    Scaling laws and stability (Parcae), constant-memory KV (MELT), depth-adaptive serving (continuous depth batching), the sparsity requirement (Looped-MoE), test-time-scaling collapse and its fix (STARS), latent-step RL (LoopRPT), and the question of what should loop at all (MixerLoop).

  9. Sep 2026
    A frontier model, reportedly

    The Information reports that Astra is a looped transformer. Which brings us to the argument.

#What is actually known about Astra

Here I want to be careful, because the tiers of evidence are being quoted interchangeably and they are not remotely equal. I could not open OpenAI's own documents, so everything below is labelled by how I got it.

  1. On the record, from OpenAI

    Jakub Pachocki, quoted in the discussion:

    "The depth of the computation graph for our present frontier models, including Astra, is within a factor of two of GPT-4."

    He also said that chain-of-thought monitoring is "fragile and unfortunately trending in a negative direction, for reasons not contingent on architecture changes." Both are posts on X, not a model card. OpenAI's "Path to Astra" document is cited as saying they are "deploying Astra with additional chain-of-thought monitoring."

  2. Journalism

    The Information reported that Astra uses a looped transformer, with an approach "similar to the one Geiping et al. introduced" in the February 2025 paper. This is the origin of the entire story, and there is no official architecture disclosure behind it.

  3. Inference, and numbers I could not check

    Rauno Arike, in the LessWrong post that started the discussion, infers three to four loops from Pachocki's statement.

    A commenter quoting Astra's deployment safety report reports that Astra "successfully controls 60.9%" on a chain-of-thought controllability evaluation against "16.1% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 1.7% for GPT-5.5 Thinking", including an ability to pretend to reason about a different question, and that UK AISI measured Astra's no-chain-of-thought maths time horizon at "30.9 minutes compared to 3.6 minutes for GPT 5.6 Sol." I have not verified either figure against the report itself, and you should discount them accordingly.

A consistency check worth doing

Those two families of numbers look contradictory at first: a modest change in depth, an enormous change in capability. They are not. Arike's own earlier work is quoted as finding that "to double a model's no-CoT time horizon, layer count needs to be increased only by 1.3×."

Take the reported time horizons at face value. 30.9 / 3.6 is 8.6×, which is log2(8.6) = 3.1 doublings, which at 1.3× per doubling needs 1.33.1 = 2.3× the layers. That sits just outside "within a factor of two", but close enough that both statements can be roughly true at once. The arithmetic is mine, not theirs, and it rests on numbers I could not verify.

The takeaway is the one that should change how you read the rest of the argument: a small multiple of depth buys a large multiple of single-pass reasoning. That is simultaneously why looping is attractive and why a few extra loops are not obviously a small thing.

The fact that reframed the thread for me

Loop counts are going down as models get bigger, not up. Huginn used 32 loops at 3.5 B. The 20 B model referred to in Arike's post as Loopie reportedly uses two. Nanbeige4.2-3B uses two passes. As the literature summary in that post puts it, "a small loop count is currently the compute-efficient regime."

So the near-term picture is two to four loops on frontier models, not thirty-two, and certainly not the millions one commenter predicted. That deflates the immediate alarm while leaving Greenblatt's version completely intact, because the thing holding loop counts down is a compute-efficiency argument, and compute-efficiency arguments move.

#If you want to touch it

Both serious pretrained looped models are open, which is the nicest thing about this corner of the field. Huginn-0125 is on Hugging Face with training code at seal-rg/recurrent-pretraining, and Ouro publishes 1.4 B and 2.6 B weights. Between them you can reproduce most of what is above on a single node.

Four things are worth an afternoon, in this order. Sweep test-time r past the training mean and find where your task peaks, because it will peak. Turn on the KL exit criterion and log per-token exit depth against token identity, which is the most legible thing this architecture produces and the closest thing it has to a readable trace. Vary the gradient window k and watch what it costs you. Then remove the input injection and watch the whole thing fall apart, which is the fastest way to believe that the anchor was doing the work.
